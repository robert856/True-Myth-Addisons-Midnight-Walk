01

Artist Statement

As a professional designer whose work has shaped luxury collections for historic estates like the Biltmore and Hearst Castle, I bring a love of visual spectacle and storytelling to the stage. Years designing sets and staging movement for community theater, including Narnia adaptations and the world premiere of The Voyage of the Dawn Treader, showed me where my real passion lies: creating bold, imaginative new plays for audiences raised on Harry Potter and Percy Jackson, who already know that a good story can hold both mystery and truth. Addison's Midnight Walk, my first, follows C. S. Lewis, J. R. R. Tolkien, and Hugo Dyson through the night that changed one of them forever, weaving high fantasy and historical fact into a single question: can myth be more than a story we tell ourselves? I build worlds, both historical and fantastical, that awaken wonder, challenge perspective, and remind audiences that the search for truth still matters today.

02

Logline

Target: 25–50 words

C.S. Lewis loves myths, but believes they are only “lies breathed through silver.” Until one extraordinary night with J.R.R. Tolkien and Hugo Dyson forces him to consider an unsettling possibility: what if the Christian myth actually happened?

03

Pitch

Target: 100–300 words

Addison's Midnight Walk follows three real men — C. S. Lewis, J. R. R. Tolkien, and Hugo Dyson — through a single day in October 1931, from the early morning hours until late into the night, moving from three simultaneous Oxford classrooms to a train platform, a pub, Lewis's college suite, and finally the garden path that gives the play its title. Lewis is a brilliant medievalist who loves myth and refuses to believe any of it is true. Dyson needles him for the contradiction. Then Tolkien, who has spent his life inventing mythologies, makes the argument that will not let Lewis go: that myths carry real truth by their nature — and that the Christian story is the one case where the myth also happened, in history, with muddy feet. Above the Oxford of lecture halls and pubs, a second stage lives entirely in image: fairies serve as stagehands until a shadowy counterforce begins to encroach, and a second Lewis, Sceptic Lewis, haunts every scene, arguing back. The wind rises on Addison's Walk. The night runs past three in the morning. Nothing is resolved on stage — but the last image sends Lewis off into the dark changed, and a short closing note tells the audience what happened nine days later, on the road to Whipsnade Zoo. The play is built entirely from the documented record: Lewis's own letters, Tolkien's poem "Mythopoeia," and Humphrey Carpenter's account in The Inklings — a true story about the moment a myth stopped being merely a story.

04

Premise

Target: 15–30 words

Three Oxford dons walk a moonlit path arguing myth against truth — and the argument itself becomes the seed of one man's religious conversion.

05

Synopsis

The stage exists in two registers simultaneously. In the foreground, Oxford 1931. In the background, a mythical realm pulses with life as fairies serve as stagehands, moving set pieces in a choreographed dance. As the night deepens, a shadowy counterforce encroaches on their work. By the final act, the audience cannot be certain whether they are watching fairies at play or angels locked in cosmic battle. A second C. S. Lewis, Sceptic Lewis, haunts the edges of every scene, whispering doubts into his own ear.

The play opens in three classrooms at once. Lewis performs Sir Gawain and the Green Knight for his students, teaching the difference between appearing virtuous and truly being virtuous. Tolkien conjures Beowulf with quiet authority, his voice dropping as he speaks of monsters as if they are real. Dyson plays every part of A Midsummer Night's Dream simultaneously, including a magnificent Nick Bottom, and his classroom erupts in laughter. Beneath the comedy he plants a seed: enchantment, transformation, the strange and irrational power of love.

The classrooms dissolve into a train platform. Dyson travels from Reading to Oxford, where Lewis meets him, and the two launch immediately into affectionate verbal combat, each trying to out-wit the other with equal delight and equal stubbornness. At the Eagle and Child pub, regulars murmur over their pints in the background as a newspaper left on a nearby table carries word of gains in the German parliament. The ribbing between Lewis and Dyson gives way to something more serious. They talk about the Nazi rise, sensing a darkness gathering that they cannot yet name. The conversation turns to Logical Positivism and the fashionable new argument that only what can be measured is real. Tolkien arrives and is invited back to Magdalen for a late supper. Before they leave, a young Canadian student tracks Lewis down to the pub, to ask a question about an assignment. His outsider question about the decline of the British Empire cracks open a moment of unexpected honesty in all three men. They are living, they realize, in the twilight of a world.

Back in Lewis's suite, over a late supper, Tolkien makes his move. Myth, he argues, is not primitive error or childish fantasy. The recurring stories across every human culture, of monsters and dying gods and miraculous return, are not lies. They are signals pointing toward something that ordinary language cannot contain. Lewis pushes back hard. Sceptic Lewis is on his feet, triumphant. Tolkien does not flinch.

Past midnight the three men walk out into Addison's Walk, a garden path beside the river Cherwell. The night is warm, still, and beautiful, the kind of evening that feels like a gift. Then without warning a great rush of wind sweeps through the garden, bending the trees, filling the air with a sound like something enormous passing overhead. They look to the sky, certain a storm is coming. On stage the cosmic battle breaks fully into the open. Sceptic Lewis stands inches from Lewis, telling him everything he stands to lose. Then the wind stops as suddenly as it came. No storm arrives. The garden returns to its perfect, uncanny calm. Lewis cannot say what has shifted, but something has. He is not where he was.

Sceptic Lewis steps back. Dyson laughs. Tolkien says nothing. A single figure, fairy or angel, crosses the stage slowly as the lights fade.

Outline

The True Myth — Outline

Setting: Oxford, England — Saturday, September 19, 1931

  • 1

    Three Classrooms

    • C. S. Lewis — Sir Gawain and the Green Knight
      • Honor, temptation, courage, truth, and the difference between appearing virtuous and truly being virtuous.
      • Establish Lewis as brilliant, confident, witty, and skeptical.
    • J. R. R. Tolkien — Beowulf
      • Monsters, heroism, mortality, sacrifice, civilization versus darkness.
      • Establish Tolkien’s belief that myth and fantastic stories can reveal truth.
    • Hugo Dyson — A Midsummer Night’s Dream

    • Dyson travels from Reading to Oxford.

  • 3

    • Lewis and Dyson meet for drinks and lunch.
    • Friendly ribbing, teasing, and attempts to one-up one another.
    • Conversation turns toward politics.
    • Rise of the Nazis in the German parliament and troubling developments in Europe.
    • Discussion of the British Empire, Dominions, and its gradual transformation.
    • Canadian student provides a personal connection to the Empire/Commonwealth discussion.
    • Conversation moves toward philosophy—reason, truth, Logical Positivism, and/or Continental philosophy.
    • Tolkien is encountered.
    • Lewis invites Tolkien to his rooms for a late dinner.
  • 4

    Lewis’s Rooms

    • Tolkien arrives.
    • Drinks, dinner, pipes, books, academic conversation, and humor.
    • Earlier political and philosophical ideas continue.
    • Conversation gradually turns toward literature.
    • Fairies, monsters, legends, ancient stories, and mythology.
    • Lewis admits his love of myth while maintaining that myths are ultimately inventions.
    • Tolkien challenges Lewis’s distinction between myth and truth.
    • Major turning point: the discussion becomes personal for Lewis.
    • Lewis’s skepticism/pride begins to manifest through Skeptic Lewis.
    • The three decide to continue their conversation outside.
  • 5

    Addison’s Walk

    • Lewis, Tolkien, and Dyson walk through the grounds of Magdalen late at night.
    • Tolkien develops his argument about myth, imagination, and truth.
    • Human beings create stories because they themselves are created beings.
    • Christianity contains the qualities Lewis loves in mythology.
    • The central proposition emerges: Christianity is a myth—but a myth that actually happened.
    • Lewis resists intellectually.
    • Skeptic Lewis increasingly challenges him internally.
    • A sudden wind moves through the trees.
    • The natural world and elements of the myths encountered earlier seem to intrude subtly upon the scene.
    • The external debate becomes Lewis’s internal struggle.
    • Lewis confronts his pride, skepticism, reason, imagination, and longing.
    • He does not suddenly convert; instead, something has fundamentally shifted.
    • Lewis allows himself to ask the question he could not ask at the beginning: Could it be true?
    • Blackout.

Characters

The Three

  • C. S. Lewis

    Oxford medievalist and self-declared rationalist. He loves myth and refuses to believe any of it is true, until the argument of this one night starts to change his mind.

  • J. R. R. Tolkien

    Philologist and lifelong builder of invented mythologies. He makes the case that will not let Lewis go: that myth can carry literal truth, and that Christianity is the one myth that also happened, in history.

  • Hugo Dyson

    Anglican wit and provocateur, warm but sharp. He keeps needling Lewis toward the question Lewis has spent years avoiding.

The Double

  • Sceptic Lewis

    A second Lewis, physically embodying his doubt on stage. He haunts every scene, whispering counterarguments in his own ear, growing more desperate as the night turns against him.

The Ensemble

  • The Fairies

    Stagehands who move the set in a choreographed dance behind the human scenes. As the night deepens, a shadowy counterforce encroaches on their work, until the audience can no longer be certain whether they're watching fairies at play or angels locked in cosmic battle.

  • The Students

    The three simultaneous classrooms are never empty. Lewis's, Tolkien's, and Dyson's students witness each lecture in a different register, solemn for Gawain, hushed for Beowulf, erupting into laughter for Dyson's Midsummer, and their reactions are the audience's first cue to how each myth is meant to land.

  • The Pub Regulars

    Unnamed patrons and the barkeep at the Eagle and Child, present in the background as Lewis, Dyson, and later Tolkien talk. A newspaper left on a nearby table, headlining gains in the German parliament, lets the gathering darkness the three men sense enter the room before anyone names it.

Also Appearing

  • The Canadian Student

    Tracks Lewis down at the pub to ask about an assignment. His outsider's question about the decline of the British Empire cracks open a moment of unexpected honesty in all three men.

  • Edith Morley

    The first woman to hold a professorial chair at an English university, appointed Professor of English Language at Reading in 1908 and still holding that post in 1931. As he leaves his classroom on his way to catch the train, Dyson runs into her in the university hallway. He drops his books; she helps him gather them, and they share a brief exchange before he goes.

Scenes

The True Myth — Scene Outline

Saturday, September 19, 1931 — Oxford

The play follows C. S. Lewis through a single day in which literature, politics, philosophy, friendship, and myth gradually converge into a question he can no longer keep at an intellectual distance: What if the myth he longs for is actually true?

Act IThe World of Ideas
  • 1

    Lewis’s Classroom: Sir Gawain and the Green Knight

    • Lewis lectures on Sir Gawain and the Green Knight: honor, temptation, courage, truth, and the difference between appearing virtuous and actually being virtuous.
    • The classroom should establish Lewis immediately: brilliant, funny, confident, combative, and intellectually in command.
    • But the subject quietly introduces the play’s deeper question: Can a man really know himself?
    • Skeptic/Prideful Lewis might make his first subtle appearance here.

    PurposeIntroduce Lewis and establish the theme of appearance versus truth.

  • 2

    Tolkien’s Classroom: Beowulf

    • Tolkien lectures on Beowulf—Grendel, Grendel’s mother, the dragon, heroism, mortality, sacrifice, and civilization’s struggle against darkness.
    • Rather than treating monsters as childish inventions, Tolkien argues that the monsters are essential to the meaning of the story.

    PurposeEstablish Tolkien’s belief that myth and fantastic stories can communicate profound truths.

  • 3

    Dyson’s Classroom: A Midsummer Night’s Dream

    • Hugo Dyson teaches Shakespeare with theatrical energy and humor.
    • Nick Bottom’s transformation and Titania’s absurd love provide the most comic of the three classroom sequences.
    • Dyson ends class early because he must catch a train to Oxford.

    PurposeIntroduce Dyson as the theatrical, witty counterweight to Lewis and Tolkien while establishing transformation as another theme.

Act IICollision of Ideas
  • 4

    The Journey to Oxford

    • Dyson rushes from his classroom, books in hand, and encounters Edith Morley before departing Reading.
    • He takes the train toward Oxford.
    • The staging can transition rapidly from classroom → station → train → Oxford.

    PurposeMove the separate worlds of the three professors toward their meeting.

  • 5

    The Eagle and Child: Friendship

    • Lewis meets Dyson at the Eagle and Child.
    • The scene begins lightly: beer, food, academic gossip, insults, teasing, and competitive one-upmanship.
    • We get to enjoy their friendship before the intellectual arguments begin.

    PurposeEstablish the personal relationship so the later philosophical debate doesn’t feel like three professors delivering lectures.

  • 6

    The Eagle and Child: Politics and Philosophy

    • Their joking gradually becomes serious.
    • The conversation moves through the changing British Empire and the Dominions, perhaps prompted by the Canadian student.
    • From there they turn toward political instability in Europe and the growing Nazi presence in German politics.
    • That leads naturally to a larger philosophical question: What happens to civilization when reason, morality, tradition, and truth cease to have an objective foundation?
    • Logical Positivism or another contemporary philosophical movement can enter here, but primarily as something the characters argue about rather than lecture about.

    PurposeExpand the play from literature into the intellectual crisis of their age.

  • 7

    Tolkien

    • Tolkien unexpectedly encounters Lewis and Dyson at the pub.
    • The tone becomes lighter again.
    • Lewis invites Tolkien to come to his rooms for a late dinner. Tolkien accepts.
    • This should feel almost inconsequential—three academics deciding to continue talking.
    • The audience, however, is about to discover that this ordinary invitation will lead to the extraordinary conversation at the heart of the play.

    PurposeBring the three men together and launch the central story.

Act IIIMyth
  • 8

    Lewis’s Rooms: Arrival and Dinner

    • Night. Tolkien arrives at Lewis’s rooms.
    • Food, drinks, pipes, books, papers, and comfortable academic disorder.
    • The men continue their joking and earlier conversations.
    • Politics and philosophy may briefly resurface, but gradually the conversation moves toward the subjects that genuinely fascinate them: Fairies. Dragons. Monsters. Legends. Gods. Myths.
    • Lewis admits the strange contradiction at the center of his thinking: he loves mythology but regards Christianity differently.

    PurposeBring Lewis’s intellectual position into direct conflict with something he deeply desires.

  • 9

    Lewis’s Rooms: The Argument About Myth

    • This is the major turn of the play.
    • Lewis argues that myths may be beautiful, powerful, even necessary—but ultimately they are inventions.
    • Tolkien challenges him. Why should something cease to communicate truth merely because it is expressed through myth?
    • The argument becomes increasingly personal. Lewis’s intellectual defenses begin to weaken.
    • Skeptic/Prideful Lewis becomes increasingly present.
    • Eventually the discussion can no longer remain confined to Lewis’s rooms. The three men decide to walk.

    PurposeMove the question from “What is myth?” to “Could Christianity be both myth and fact?”

Act IVAddison’s Walk
  • 10

    The Walk

    • Lewis, Tolkien, and Dyson walk beneath the trees around Magdalen.
    • The intellectual argument continues, but its character changes. This is no longer a university debate.
    • Tolkien develops the idea that human beings create myths because they themselves are creations—and therefore their stories can contain fragments of a greater truth.
    • Christianity, he suggests, possesses the qualities Lewis loves in mythology, but with one extraordinary difference: The myth happened.
    • Lewis resists. Skeptic/Prideful Lewis confronts him.

    PurposeBring the external philosophical argument inside Lewis himself.

  • 11

    Wind

    • The famous sudden wind becomes theatrical.
    • Leaves move. Branches shake. The peaceful walk changes.
    • The world around Lewis seems almost to participate in the argument.
    • The mythic imagery encountered throughout the play—the Green Knight, Beowulf’s monsters, Shakespeare’s enchanted forest—can subtly intrude upon the real world.
    • Not literally proving anything. Rather, Lewis’s imagination and reason are beginning to occupy the same world.

    PurposeMove from intellectual argument to mystery.

  • 12

    Lewis’s Confrontation

    • Tolkien and Dyson move ahead or disappear into darkness. Lewis remains.
    • Skeptic/Prideful Lewis confronts him directly.
    • Everything established throughout the play now returns:
      • Gawain: What is the difference between appearing truthful and being truthful?
      • Beowulf: Are monsters merely inventions because they cannot be measured?
      • Shakespeare: Can transformation occur when someone sees the world differently?
      • Politics/philosophy: What happens when reason is separated from meaning?
      • Myth: Why does Lewis hunger for something he insists cannot be true?
    • And finally Lewis faces the question he has spent the entire play avoiding: Could it be true?
    • The play doesn’t need to portray his full conversion that night—historically, it wasn’t that immediate.
    • Instead, the victory is smaller and dramatically more interesting: Lewis becomes willing to consider the possibility. The intellectual door he has kept firmly closed has opened.
    • Blackout.

Sample Dialogue

Empire or Commonwealth Wyrd Take A
Empire or Commonwealth

The Eagle and Child — Oxford, 1931

Late afternoon. The Eagle and Child is warm and crowded. Low conversation, clinking glasses, tobacco smoke. LEWIS and DYSON sit at a small wooden table with pints before them. A newspaper lies folded nearby.

A young Oxford STUDENT passes their table carrying a book. He recognizes Lewis and hesitates.

STUDENT Excuse me, Mr. Lewis.

LEWIS Ah, yes. Come along. You’re—?

STUDENT Thompson, sir. From Canada. I had a question about the essay—

DYSON (grinning) From the colonies, eh? Welcome to the center of the universe.

STUDENT (with a polite half-smile) So I’ve been told, sir. Though we don’t quite call it that anymore.

A small pause. Lewis notices.

LEWIS No? What do you call it?

STUDENT A Dominion, sir.

DYSON A very grand word for the same arrangement, I should think.

STUDENT If you don’t mind, sir… it doesn’t feel the same.

That lands. TOLKIEN, approaching the table with a pint, catches the last remark.

TOLKIEN In what way?

STUDENT Well… we govern ourselves. Make our own laws. It’s still the King, of course—but… (choosing his words carefully) It doesn’t feel like belonging to Britain. More like… belonging with it.

A quiet shift around the table.

DYSON With it? Until you decide otherwise, I imagine.

STUDENT Perhaps, sir. But isn’t that the point?

Lewis studies him, intrigued.

LEWIS And if you did decide otherwise?

STUDENT Then it ought to be our decision to make.

A beat. No one immediately answers.

TOLKIEN (softly) That is a dangerous kind of freedom.

STUDENT Yes, sir. (a slight smile) But it’s still freedom.

LEWIS Quite. And the essay?

STUDENT Tomorrow, sir?

LEWIS Tomorrow.

STUDENT Thank you, sir.

The Student moves off through the pub. Dyson watches him go, then takes a long drink.

DYSON Well. There it is. The Empire, politely walking out the door.

LEWIS Or perhaps simply growing up.

Tolkien pulls out a chair and joins them.

LEWIS It seems to me the word “Empire” is already a ghost. We’re simply pretending not to notice.

DYSON (laughing dryly) A ghost? Hardly. It still collects taxes, commands ships, and sends young men abroad. Ghosts don’t do that.

TOLKIEN Names matter, though. Empires claim dominion. A Commonwealth—if it means anything—suggests fellowship. That is no small difference.

DYSON Fellowship? That’s a very Oxford way of dressing up retreat. Call it what it is—we’re loosening our grip because we can’t hold it.

LEWIS Or because we shouldn’t.

A pause. Dyson studies him.

DYSON You think Britain has no right to lead?

LEWIS I think leadership freely given is one thing… and rule imposed is quite another. The former might endure. The latter always cracks.

TOLKIEN (nodding) History agrees. All empires believe themselves permanent. None are.

DYSON So we simply… step aside? Hand everything over and hope for the best?

LEWIS Not “hope.” Trust. There’s a difference. If these nations are to be truly themselves, they must choose their own path—even if it leads away from us.

DYSON (grimly) And what becomes of Britain then?

TOLKIEN Perhaps something older. Smaller, yes—but truer.

DYSON Or weaker.

LEWIS (smiling faintly) Those are not always the same thing.

Another pause. Around them, the ordinary life of the pub continues.

DYSON I don’t like it. Feels like we’re watching the sun set—and calling it progress.

TOLKIEN Every sunset is also a beginning, if you’re willing to wait through the dark.

DYSON You’ve been reading too many myths.

LEWIS (on the edge of a grin) Or perhaps not enough.

Wyrd

Lewis’s Rooms, Magdalen College — Night

Books everywhere. Dinner has ended badly, which is to say successfully. Plates remain on the table. Pipes have been lit. A bottle stands between them.

TOLKIEN has a book open on his knee.

LEWIS You cannot possibly maintain that Beowulf is improved by knowing precisely which mud-hole in Schleswig-Holstein produced the word for “monster.”

TOLKIEN I did not say Schleswig-Holstein.

LEWIS You were approaching it.

DYSON I could see Schleswig-Holstein coming over the horizon.

TOLKIEN What I said was that a word carries its history with it.

LEWIS Words carry what we put into them.

TOLKIEN No. That is precisely the modern error.

LEWIS Oh, good. We’ve discovered the modern error.

DYSON And before pudding.

TOLKIEN Take wyrd.

DYSON Must we?

TOLKIEN Yes, Hugo. We must.

LEWIS Fate.

TOLKIEN Not fate.

LEWIS Oh, come.

TOLKIEN Not in the classical sense. Wyrd is what has become—what has happened—and therefore what confronts a man now. It comes from weorþan: to become.

LEWIS So when Beowulf says—

TOLKIEN “Gæð a wyrd swa hio scel.”

LEWIS There. That.

DYSON Perfectly clear. I don’t know why translators bother.

LEWIS “Fate goes ever as fate must.”

TOLKIEN Approximately.

LEWIS Approximately? You philologists are insufferable. You spend twenty years discovering what a word means and then refuse to tell anyone.

TOLKIEN Because you want it to mean one thing.

LEWIS Words generally do.

TOLKIEN Bad words do.

DYSON And German words mean six things at once and occupy half the page.

TOLKIEN Wyrd contains an entire conception of existence. The past has become the present. A man finds himself within it. He cannot undo it. He can only decide how he shall meet it.

A beat.

LEWIS That I like.

DYSON Of course you do. It sounds like you.

LEWIS How does it sound like me?

DYSON Three parts free will, two parts tragedy, and a great deal of shouting.

LEWIS I do not shout.

DYSON Jack, you order breakfast as though opposing a motion in Parliament.

Tolkien laughs.

LEWIS All right. But here is my objection. You are smuggling philosophy into a poem.

TOLKIEN No. The philosophy is already in the poem.

LEWIS Because you find it there.

TOLKIEN Because the poet put it there.

LEWIS Did he?

TOLKIEN Perhaps not consciously.

LEWIS Ah!

TOLKIEN That does not mean it isn’t there.

LEWIS Ronald, that is intellectual burglary. You break into the poet’s house, deposit your own furniture, and then congratulate him on his taste.

DYSON I’ve seen his rooms. Tolkien’s furniture would improve them.

TOLKIEN You both make the mistake of assuming that a story belongs entirely to the man who tells it.

LEWIS Whom else should it belong to?

TOLKIEN The language. The tradition. All the stories that came before it.

LEWIS And I suppose the fairies.

TOLKIEN Especially the fairies.

DYSON There we are.

LEWIS I knew we’d get to fairies eventually.

DYSON Give him another glass and we’ll have dragons by midnight.

TOLKIEN You laugh, but a dragon is not merely an oversized lizard.

LEWIS No, obviously. It has capital.

DYSON And property.

LEWIS Usually stolen.

TOLKIEN The dragon is greed—

LEWIS There! Now who’s smuggling philosophy into the poem?

TOLKIEN I was going to say the dragon is greed made visible.

Lewis stops.

That lands.

LEWIS Made visible.

TOLKIEN Yes.

LEWIS Now that’s different.

DYSON Why?

LEWIS Because an allegory says, “This dragon represents greed.” That’s dull. But if greed somehow becomes a dragon—

TOLKIEN Precisely.

LEWIS —then you haven’t explained greed.

TOLKIEN No.

LEWIS You’ve made me see it.

TOLKIEN And fear it.

LEWIS And perhaps desire it.

DYSON You desire the dragon?

LEWIS The gold, you ass.

DYSON I was becoming concerned.

Lewis rises, warming to the thought.

LEWIS That’s what the old stories do, isn’t it? They take something we know perfectly well—

TOLKIEN Or think we know.

LEWIS —and estrange it. Make it strange enough that we can see it again.

TOLKIEN Yes.

LEWIS A wood is only a wood until someone tells you there are elves in it.

DYSON Then it becomes a wood containing irritating little people.

TOLKIEN Not elves like that.

DYSON There are categories?

TOLKIEN Of course there are categories.

DYSON God help us.

TOLKIEN Don’t bring Him into it. Jack will object.

LEWIS I do not object to God.

DYSON Progress.

LEWIS I object to what people say about Him.

TOLKIEN Also progress.

LEWIS Oh, shut up.

They laugh.

Tolkien closes the book.

TOLKIEN But you’ve just admitted something important.

LEWIS I have admitted nothing.

TOLKIEN You said a story can make us see something true.

LEWIS Certainly.

TOLKIEN Without stating it as a proposition.

LEWIS Yes.

TOLKIEN And perhaps more powerfully because it does not.

Lewis realizes where this is going.

LEWIS Careful, Ronald.

DYSON He’s laying track.

LEWIS I can see the locomotive.

TOLKIEN Then perhaps you should stop standing on the rails.

LEWIS Myths may contain truth. I grant you that. They express human longings, fears, desires—

TOLKIEN Why merely human?

A pause.

LEWIS There.

DYSON And we have arrived.

LEWIS No. We have arrived nowhere. We were discussing Beowulf.

DYSON We were discussing one Anglo-Saxon word.

LEWIS Even better.

DYSON And somehow we have reached God.

TOLKIEN That’s what happens when you follow words carefully.

DYSON Then tomorrow we discuss punctuation.

LEWIS Absolutely not.

DYSON Excellent. By breakfast we’ll have settled the nature of the universe.

TOLKIEN Not before breakfast.

LEWIS Why not?

TOLKIEN I should like another drink first.

Lewis considers him, then reaches for the bottle.

LEWIS For once tonight, Ronald, you’ve said something entirely reasonable.

BLACKOUT.

Take A

Addison’s Walk — Magdalen College

Oxford, 1931. Late evening, after dinner. LEWIS, TOLKIEN, and DYSON stroll along the path, pipes in hand, autumn leaves underfoot.

DYSON Jack, you pagan Viking, still clutching your Norse myths like a schoolboy with his conkers. One day that thunder-god hammer of yours is going to drop straight on that thick Ulster skull.

LEWIS And you, Hugo, with your Anglican incense and bells—always trying to baptize every decent story into the font. Next you’ll tell me Beowulf secretly longed for a bishopric.

DYSON Better a bishop than a dour Ulster atheist playing at being a medievalist. You lecture on courtly love by day and moon over dying gods by night. Make up your mind, man! Are you a rationalist or a romantic? You can’t have it both ways; it’s intellectually indecent.

LEWIS (chuckling) Indecent? Says the man who talks more in one evening than most fellows manage in a term. You’re a roaring cataract of nonsense, Dyson. If eloquence were scholarship, you’d be Master of the College by now.

DYSON Flattery from you? Dangerous. I’d sooner trust a Socratic daemon. But seriously, old thing—your precious myths stir the blood, yet you insist they’re all lies. What if one of them happened? What if the True Myth came down and got its feet muddy right here in the dirt of history?

LEWIS (grunting) Now you sound like Tolkien with his elves. Muddy feet? Next you’ll have the Almighty filing a change of address at the Bodleian.

DYSON Better muddy feet than your bloodless abstractions! You enjoy the idea of sacrifice in your sagas, but the real thing makes you squeamish. Typical Lewis—wants the poetry without the peril. Come now, admit it: deep down you’re half-convinced already. I can see the crack in that rationalist armour.

LEWIS Half-convinced by a pair of enthusiastic Jesuits-in-waiting? I’d sooner be converted by a committee of Welsh Methodists. At least they sing better.

DYSON (laughing heartily) Touché. But mark me, Jack—if the wind rises tonight and the leaves dance like something out of Malory, don’t blame me. Even the weather’s on my side.

LEWIS Insufferable. Let’s walk another round before you start quoting Scripture at me. I’ve had quite enough evangelism for one pipe.

They continue, trading barbs into the small hours.
